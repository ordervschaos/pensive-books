# Non-Fiction Evaluation Criteria

A practical rubric for assessing a work of non-fiction. Non-fiction asserts that its contents are factually true and useful; therefore, evaluation must test both the **validity of the underlying argument** and the **structural presentation** of that argument.

The sub-genre matters: a systemic argument (policy, science, history) is judged differently than a self-help book, a memoir, or an educational text. Identify the sub-category before scoring, and apply the relevant sub-rubric in addition to the core criteria.

---

## 1. Argument & Thesis

### 1.1 Thesis Clarity
- **Question:** Can the central argument be identified within a few minutes of browsing the table of contents, preface, and introduction?
- **Positive signals:** A single, articulable thesis; explicit framing of what the book claims and what it does not.
- **Red flags:** No identifiable thesis; the book reads as a compilation of facts rather than an argument.

### 1.2 Organizational Structure
- **Question:** Is the argument deliberately structured — building from premises to conclusions — rather than associatively assembled?
- **Positive signals:** Chapter sequence has a logic; each chapter advances the central claim; transitions are explicit.
- **Red flags:** Random ordering; chapters that could be shuffled without loss; repetition that masquerades as development.

### 1.3 Scope Discipline
- **Question:** Is the scope appropriate to the length and the author's expertise?
- **Positive signals:** The book commits to what it can support; acknowledged limitations are honest, not evasive.
- **Red flags:** Overreach beyond the evidence; topic sprawl; or conversely, a book-length argument that should have been an essay.

---

## 2. Logical Rigor

### 2.1 Logic vs. Emotion Balance
- **Question:** Does the argument rely on sound logic, or does it substitute emotional rhetoric for reasoning?
- **Positive signals:** A degree of authorial passion is acceptable; arguments stand on their own structure when emotion is stripped out.
- **Red flags:** Emotional appeals carrying load-bearing weight; outrage or sentiment used to short-circuit analysis.

### 2.2 Treatment of Counter-Arguments
- **Question:** Does the author systematically engage competing hypotheses?
- **Positive signals:** Steelmanned counter-positions; counter-evidence acknowledged and addressed; uncertainty expressed where it exists.
- **Red flags:** Strawmen; selective citation; dismissal of opposing views without engagement.

### 2.3 Causal Reasoning
- **Question:** Are claims about cause and effect grounded — or are correlations dressed as causation?
- **Positive signals:** Explicit causal mechanisms; awareness of confounders.
- **Red flags:** Just-so stories; post-hoc reasoning; sweeping causal claims from anecdotes.

---

## 3. Evidence & Methodology

### 3.1 Empirical Support
- **Question:** Does the book prove its central thesis with evidence, or merely assert it?
- **Positive signals:** Data, primary sources, case studies, or experimental results back the argument. Example: *Guns, Germs, and Steel* systematically tests competing hypotheses with historical evidence.
- **Red flags:** The "we must do this" failure pattern — proposing a sweeping plan without evidence that society can or will execute it (e.g., *Earth in the Balance* is critiqued for this).

### 3.2 Source Quality
- **Question:** How was the data obtained? Are the sources reliable?
- **Positive signals:** Primary sources; peer-reviewed studies; transparent methodology; full citations.
- **Red flags:** Unsourced statistics; recycled secondary citations; selective use of outlier studies.

### 3.3 Methodological Transparency
- **Question:** Can the reader trace how the author arrived at their conclusions?
- **Positive signals:** Methodology is stated; data and reasoning are inspectable.
- **Red flags:** Conclusions arrive without showing work; the reader is asked to trust without verification.

### 3.4 Comparative Context
- **Question:** How does this book compare to other publications on the same subject?
- **Positive signals:** The author engages the existing literature; situates their contribution; identifies what is genuinely new.
- **Red flags:** Treats the topic as if no one has written about it before; ignores adjacent or competing works.

---

## 4. Authorial Credibility

### 4.1 The Two Lookup Tests
This is one of the most efficient heuristics for assessing a non-fiction author.

- **Lookup Test 1 (Accuracy):** Pick a topic in the book you already know deeply. Does the author get it right?
- **Lookup Test 2 (Explanatory Effectiveness):** Pick a topic in the book you want to learn. Can the author explain it clearly?
- **Positive signals:** Passes both — accurate on what you know, illuminating on what you don't.
- **Red flags:** Errors in your area of expertise; confused or shallow explanations in unfamiliar territory.

### 4.2 Platform & Qualifications
- **Question:** Does the author have the standing — credentials, experience, or demonstrated work — to speak on this subject?
- **Positive signals:** Direct expertise; rigorous adjacent qualification; transparent disclosure of perspective.
- **Red flags:** Unsupported authority claims; ghostwritten celebrity expertise without credentialed collaborator.

### 4.3 Bias Disclosure
- **Question:** Are conflicts of interest, ideological commitments, or institutional ties disclosed?
- **Positive signals:** Author names their perspective and its limits.
- **Red flags:** Hidden funding; undisclosed agendas; framing presented as neutral when it isn't.

---

## 5. Depth: Root-Cause vs. Surface

### 5.1 Symptom vs. Cause
- **Question:** Does the book address root systemic causes, or stop at proximate symptoms?
- **Positive signals:** The argument penetrates to underlying drivers; treats the problem as a system rather than an event.
- **Red flags:** Lists of symptoms with no mechanism; technical fixes proposed without accounting for the sociological, economic, or political resistance to them (e.g., *Natural Capitalism* is critiqued for ignoring human-behavior resistance).

### 5.2 Systems Awareness
- **Question:** Does the author acknowledge the system in which their solution must operate?
- **Positive signals:** Considers incentives, stakeholders, feedback loops, and constraints.
- **Red flags:** Solutions presented in a vacuum; the reader is left wondering "who would actually do this, and why?"

---

## 6. Focus: The 80/20 Rule

### 6.1 High-Leverage Concentration
- **Question:** Does the book focus on the 20% of the issue that produces 80% of the impact?
- **Positive signals:** Disciplined identification of high-leverage points. Example: *Silent Spring* focuses on a single high-leverage target (DDT) rather than attempting to cover all environmental degradation.
- **Red flags:** Comprehensive coverage that dilutes the central point; chasing every adjacent topic; a book that wants to be exhaustive instead of decisive.

### 6.2 Prioritization
- **Question:** Does the author explicitly tell the reader what matters most?
- **Positive signals:** Explicit hierarchy of importance.
- **Red flags:** Every chapter framed as equally critical; the reader cannot extract a ranked takeaway.

---

## 7. Audience & Utility

### 7.1 Audience Resonance
- **Question:** Does the book transmit on a wavelength matched to the comprehension level and prior knowledge of its target reader?
- **Positive signals:** Appropriate vocabulary; assumed background is calibrated; jargon is defined when necessary.
- **Red flags:** Mismatch between expertise required and audience addressed (too technical or too patronizing).

### 7.2 Actionability
- **Question:** Can the reader do something with the contents — change a behavior, understand a phenomenon, make a decision?
- **Positive signals:** Clear takeaways; the reader leaves capable of something new.
- **Red flags:** "So what?" — the book diagnoses without enabling.

### 7.3 Prose Mechanics
- **Question:** Is the language clear, grammatically competent, and unambiguous?
- **Positive signals:** Authoritative, readable prose; sentences serve clarity over flourish.
- **Red flags:** Convoluted constructions; jargon used to obscure rather than clarify; recurring grammatical problems.

## 8. Self-Help Sub-Rubric

Apply in addition to core criteria when evaluating self-help, productivity, or transformational literature. A 2008 study of the fifty top-selling self-help books found that only 24% provided ways to measure progress, only 34% offered long-term solutions, and only 48% used evidence-backed techniques. These three failures define the genre's primary risk surface.

### 8.1 Measurability of Progress
- **Question:** Does the book give the reader a way to objectively measure whether the method is working?
- **Positive signals:** Diagnostic tools, self-assessments, before/after metrics.
- **Red flags:** Vague aspirational outcomes; no way to falsify "it's working."

### 8.2 Long-Term Orientation
- **Question:** Does the book pursue durable behavioral change, or quick emotional boosts?
- **Positive signals:** Systematic, repeatable practices; habits over moments.
- **Red flags:** Motivation as a substitute for method; reliance on emotional intensity.

### 8.3 Evidence Base
- **Question:** Are the techniques backed by peer-reviewed research or rigorous practitioner experience?
- **Positive signals:** Cited studies; named methodologies; honest acknowledgment of where evidence is thin.
- **Red flags:** Anecdote as proof; misuse of neuroscience or psychology terminology; "quantum" or pseudo-scientific framing.

### 8.4 Proto-Persona Targeting
- **Question:** Is the book written for a specific reader with specific pain points — or for "everyone"?
- **Positive signals:** A clearly defined target reader; specific transformations promised; the reader can immediately answer "why does this matter to my problem?"
- **Red flags:** Audience defined as a demographic mega-segment ("this book is for Baby Boomers") with no narrower focus; commercially weak and analytically unfocused.

### 8.5 Structural Map
- **Question:** Does the book follow a coherent journey from problem identification to resolution?
- **Positive signals:** A reliable five-part (or similar) structure: diagnosis → understanding → method → practice → integration.
- **Red flags:** Loose collections of tips; chapters that don't build on each other.

## 9. Educational / Youth Sub-Rubric

Apply in addition to core criteria when evaluating textbooks, educational non-fiction, or books for young readers.

### 9.1 RADAR Model (Ontario Institute for Studies in Education)
- **Rationale** — Is the purpose of the text clear?
- **Authority** — Is the author/publisher credentialed?
- **Date** — Is the information current?
- **Accuracy** — Are facts verifiable?
- **Relevance** — Does the content match the intended audience and curriculum?

### 9.2 Scientific Integrity (for science / nature books)
- **Question:** Are scientific phenomena described accurately and without misleading projection?
- **Positive signals:** Free of anthropomorphism (animals don't "want" things they can't want); free of teleological framing (evolution doesn't have "goals"); clear distinction between observed fact and explanatory theory.
- **Red flags:** Cute but misleading attribution of human emotion to non-human subjects; presenting theory as settled fact or vice versa.

### 9.3 Representation
- **Question:** Do the text and illustrations reveal diversity and avoid stereotypes?
- **Positive signals:** Inclusive examples; varied perspectives; counter-stereotypical defaults.
- **Red flags:** Default white/male/Western framings; tokenism.

---

## 10. Memoir / Narrative Non-Fiction Notes

When the book is structured as narrative (memoir, narrative history, longform journalism), also apply the relevant criteria from the fiction rubric — character, scene, pacing, voice — while holding the work to non-fiction's standards for truth, sourcing, and the line between recollection and reconstruction.

---

## Synthesis: Overall Assessment

After scoring all relevant sections, work through the **Thwink seven-point synthesis** (Jack Harich) as a final stress test:

1. **Is the argument clear and organized** — can the thesis be stated in one sentence after reading the table of contents?
2. **Does it resonate** — is the message pitched correctly for its audience?
3. **Does logic outweigh emotion** — would the argument hold if emotional language were removed?
4. **Do the Two Lookup Tests pass** — accurate on what you know, illuminating on what you don't?
5. **Does it prove its thesis with evidence** — or does it propose action without showing the mechanism?
6. **Does it reach root causes** — or stop at surface symptoms?
7. **Does it obey 80/20** — focusing on high-leverage factors rather than attempting exhaustive coverage?

Then ask:
- **What does the reader leave able to do, understand, or decide that they could not before?**
- **If a competing expert read this, what would they correctly challenge?**
- **Is this book the best available on its subject — and if not, why does it exist?**

Maintain critical detachment. Avoid generic superlatives. Penalize a book for failing what it claims to do, not for failing to be a different book.
