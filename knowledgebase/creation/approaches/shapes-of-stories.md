# Shapes of Stories

Kurt Vonnegut's idea (from his rejected master's thesis and later lectures): a story's
emotional trajectory can be drawn as a single line on two axes —

- **Vertical:** good fortune (top) ↔ ill fortune (bottom) for the protagonist.
- **Horizontal:** beginning → end.

A handful of shapes recur across most stories. They are not templates to write *to*; they
are **diagnostic frames** for a SPINE — the whole book as one causal arc. Ask: which shape is
this book tracing? Does the spine actually draw that line, or does it go flat, wander, or
resolve on the wrong side of the axis?

> Source: Kurt Vonnegut, "The Shapes of Stories" (lecture). The PNG in this folder
> (`shapes-of-stories.png`, via HBR / David Yang) charts Man in Hole, Boy Gets Girl, and
> Cinderella ("the most popular story in Western civilization").

## The shapes

**1. Man in Hole** — *most stories.*
Starts at or above average fortune → drops into trouble → climbs out to end **higher** than it
began. "Somebody gets into trouble, gets out of it again; it is not necessarily about a man and
it is not necessarily about a hole." The fall must be real and the climb must be *earned*.
- *Catches:* a hole that isn't deep enough (no real trouble), or a protagonist who climbs out by
  luck/rescue instead of by their own change. A spine that ends exactly where it started has no
  hole — it's flat.

**2. Boy Gets Girl** — *the want-it / lose-it / regain-it curve.*
Starts at an ordinary baseline → stumbles onto something wonderful → **loses** it → gets it back
for good, ending well above baseline. The midpoint loss is the engine.
- *Catches:* a missing loss (gets the wonderful thing and simply keeps it — no middle), or a
  regaining that costs nothing. The dip has to hurt or the payoff is unearned.

**3. Cinderella / From Bad to Better** — *the staircase, the drop, the soar.*
Starts low → a series of **incremental gifts** lifts her step by step → a sudden crushing **drop**
(midnight) → then soars to off-the-chart happiness. Vonnegut noted this maps onto the New
Testament — the most reliably satisfying shape in Western storytelling.
- *Catches:* gifts that arrive all at once (no staircase), or no drop before the rise (joy with no
  jeopardy reads as flat). The higher the final soar, the deeper the drop must precede it.

**4. From Bad to Worse** — *Kafka's* Metamorphosis.
Starts below average → descends, relentlessly, with no recovery. The arc is the absence of rescue.
- *Catches:* use this to TEST a tragic spine — is the descent truly unbroken, or did you flinch and
  sneak in a consolation that softens the fall? A real downward shape commits.

**5. Which Way Is Up?** — *Hamlet; ambiguity as the shape.*
The line stays near the axis because we genuinely **can't tell** whether each event is good news or
bad news for the protagonist — and neither can they. Vonnegut admired this as the most honest shape:
life rarely announces which turns are fortunate.
- *Catches:* a spine where every beat is legibly "good" or "bad" may be too tidy. Conversely, if you
  *intend* a clear arc but the reader can't read up from down, the shape has gone slack — ambiguity
  by accident, not design.

**6. Creation / Ascending Steps** — the stepwise gift.
A monotonic rise — gift after gift, each leaving the protagonist better off, no descent. Pure
ascent. Rarely a satisfying *whole* story on its own (no jeopardy), but a useful name for an act or
a stretch that should later be cut into by a drop.
- *Catches:* if your whole spine is this shape, it has no hole and no midnight — diagnose where the
  drop belongs.

## How to use against a spine

1. **Name the shape** the book is reaching for (often Man in Hole or Cinderella).
2. **Trace the spine's line** against it: where's the fall? the midpoint turn? the final position
   relative to the start?
3. **Find the break:** a hole with no bottom, a loss that doesn't cost, a soar with no preceding
   drop, a flat middle. That break is the highest-leverage fix.

The shape never overrides the book's brief, argument, or settled ending — it only reveals where the
arc isn't yet drawing the line it's promising.
