# Writing Routines & Execution

**What it is:** The discipline layer — the daily regimens and psychological conditioning that turn a structure into a finished manuscript. There is no single standard; there's a spectrum of techniques for harnessing focus, bypassing writer's block, and staying connected to the story.

**Why it matters:** The most flawless [structure](../README.md) produces nothing without execution. This block exists because *how* you sit down to write determines whether the book gets finished — especially for [discovery writers](../approaches/discovery-writing.md), whose method depends on not losing their grip on the story.

---

## The spectrum of routines

Pick the one that fits your temperament — these are alternatives, not a checklist.

- **Metric-driven discipline.** A fixed daily word count. Hemingway wrote from 6 AM, revised yesterday's work to re-enter flow, then produced 500–1,000 new words before noon. King aims for 1,000 words a day over ~4 hours and warns that days off let characters go stale and loosen your hold on the story.
- **Psychological suspension.** Stop while you still have energy *and* know what happens next (Maya Angelou). Returning to a known next step kills the dread of the blank morning page and gives built-in momentum.
- **Rigid rule systems.** Hard behavioral constraints to eliminate distraction (Susan Sontag: wake before eight, no phone calls, limited lunches, no daytime reading — because reading was an escape from writing).
- **Unstructured passion.** Reject schedules entirely (Ray Bradbury) — be driven to the desk by the story's emotional charge. Works only when that charge is reliably there.
- **Environmental conditioning.** Use place and ritual to trigger the writing state: Peter James writes at 6 PM with a martini and specific music; Susan Barker used the white noise of foreign-language coffee shops; Joan Didion needed an hour alone with a drink before dinner to review the day's pages.

---

## How to apply it

- Match the routine to your [drafting approach](../README.md). Discovery writers benefit most from **daily continuity** (metric-driven or psychological suspension) because momentum is the method. Outliners can tolerate more stop-start, since the plan holds the story between sessions.
- Steal the **"stop while you know what's next"** trick regardless of approach — it's the cheapest defense against blank-page dread.
- Treat the word count as a floor that protects the habit, not a ceiling. The point is showing up consistently, not the number itself.

## Pitfalls

- **Borrowing the wrong writer's routine.** Bradbury's no-schedule freedom is a trap if your passion isn't reliably present; Sontag's austerity is overkill if you already focus easily.
- **Days off that compound.** For momentum-dependent drafting, gaps let the story go cold — the cost is re-immersion time, not just lost words.
