# World-Building & Magic Systems

**What it is:** A discipline for constructing the rules of a speculative setting — magic systems, advanced technologies, the physics of the impossible — so they extend the narrative structure and character psychology instead of breaking them.

**Why it matters:** In fantasy, science fiction, and supernatural thrillers, the world's rules *are* part of the plot's machinery. Undisciplined world-building lets magic solve problems for free, which collapses tension. This block governs how to keep that from happening.

**Pairs with:** Any speculative [structure](../README.md); the [Hero's Journey](../structures/heros-journey.md) in particular leans on a well-defined "Special World."

---

## Soft vs. hard magic

- **Soft magic** is wondrous and undefined — used to create awe and mystery. The reader isn't meant to understand its rules.
- **Hard magic** runs on strict, explicit rules with defined limits, which characters use to solve problems like alternative physics.

The distinction matters because of *how you're allowed to use it* — which Sanderson's Laws make precise.

## Sanderson's Laws of Magic

1. **Law One.** Your ability to resolve conflict *with* magic is proportional to how well the reader understands its rules. Solving a plot point with undefined (soft) magic reads as *deus ex machina*. Resolution must come from understood parameters.
2. **Law Two.** Limitations, costs, and weaknesses are more compelling than powers. What a character *can't* do — and what a power *costs* them — forces ingenuity. Real tension comes from depriving a character of their primary tool.
3. **Law Three.** Expand existing rules before adding new ones. Exploring the deep social, political, and psychological implications of *one* rule builds a more coherent world than piling on disconnected, shallow powers.

## Defining a system

Specify these four parameters to keep a magic or tech system consistent:

| Parameter | What it sets | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Origin of the power; dictates who controls it and the world's geopolitics. | Gods, ley lines/aether, demons, willpower, scientific reality. |
| **Activator** | The mechanism to manifest it; creates ways for foes to interrupt or disable it. | Incantations, runes, gestures, consuming rare materials. |
| **Cost & Limitations** | The balancing mechanism; the stakes of using the power. | Exhaustion, lost memories, ethical corruption, years of mastery. |
| **Manifestation** | The sensory footprint on the world and the user. | Does the user glow? Can enemies sense it from miles away? Immediate or residual? |

## How to apply it

- Decide soft vs. hard *per element* and per purpose. Use soft magic for wonder and atmosphere; use hard magic for anything that will resolve a plot point.
- Write the **limitations before the powers.** Start from what the magic *costs* and *can't* do — that's where the story's problems and ingenuity come from.
- Make the world produce the plot. If the same story could happen in a generic setting, the world-building is decoration, not structure.

## Pitfalls

- **Magic as a get-out-of-jail card.** Resolving a climax with a power the reader didn't understand violates Law One and feels like cheating.
- **Breadth over depth.** A dozen shallow powers are weaker than one rule explored to its social and ethical consequences (Law Three).
- **Inconsistency under pressure.** Rules that quietly change to suit the plot break immersion and the genre contract.
