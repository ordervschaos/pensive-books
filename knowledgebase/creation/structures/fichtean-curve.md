# The Fichtean Curve

**What it is:** A tension-first structure that skips slow setup and drops the protagonist straight into crisis, then climbs through a series of escalating crises to the climax. Rooted in Johann Gottlieb Fichte's dramatic theory; popularized for writers by John Gardner in *The Art of Fiction*.

**Best for:** Thrillers, mysteries, suspense, and pulp — any genre whose contract is relentless forward momentum and a fast-paced plot.

**Approach it pairs with:** Either. Suits [discovery writers](../approaches/discovery-writing.md) who like to chase tension, and [outliners](../approaches/outlining.md) tracking clue placement.

---

## The shape

Not a list of beats but a rising sawtooth: a wave that keeps peaking higher, dipping briefly, then peaking again.

1. **Open *in medias res*.** Plunge the protagonist into a crisis on the first pages — no traditional first-act world-building.
2. **Weave backstory into conflict.** Reveal character and setting incrementally, *inside* moments of tension, while the stakes are already escalating. Never isolate exposition.
3. **Build through escalating crises.** Three or more rising crises, each larger than the last. Between them, allow brief "mini-resolutions" — short breathing spaces that develop subplot without ever fully resetting the tension.
4. **Spike to the climax.** The accumulated tension peaks in the final, largest crisis.
5. **Compress the denouement.** Tie up loose ends quickly and get out — don't overstay the resolution.

---

## How to use it to create a book

- Your **opening line is a crisis, not a sunrise.** If your first scene establishes normalcy, you're writing a different structure.
- Keep the dips short and deliberate. They exist to let the reader breathe and to plant subplot — not to deflate the book. The tension floor should rise each time.
- For mysteries, the mini-resolutions are where clues land; track them (a [spreadsheet outline](../approaches/outlining.md) helps) so the climax pays off fairly.

## Pitfalls

- **Front-loaded exposition.** A "first act" of setup defeats the structure. Backstory must arrive mid-conflict.
- **Flat escalation.** If crisis #3 isn't bigger than crisis #1, the curve plateaus and the reader's pulse drops.
- **Bloated ending.** A long, tidy denouement undoes the momentum you spent the whole book building.
