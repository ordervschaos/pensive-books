# The Snowflake Method

**What it is:** A bottom-up outlining process that grows a novel from a single sentence outward in ten expanding passes — like a fractal snowflake. Developed by Randy Ingermanson.

**Best for:** Writers who freeze at the blank page or feel overwhelmed by scope. It paces complexity so you never build more scaffolding than you're ready for. Strong fit for plot-heavy fiction with many moving parts.

**Approach it pairs with:** [Outlining](../approaches/outlining.md). It *is* an outlining discipline.

---

## The steps

Work them in order. Each step's output becomes the input to the next, so the story stays coherent as it grows.

1. **One-sentence summary.** Write the whole novel in one sentence — under ~15 words, no character names, big picture only. This is your hook and your 10-second pitch.
2. **One-paragraph summary.** Expand the sentence into ~5 sentences: the setup, three major disasters (each roughly a quarter of the book, each caused by the protagonist's failed fix of the last), and the resolution.
3. **Character summaries.** One page per major character: name, motivation, concrete goal, conflict, and epiphany (the moment their false belief shatters — see [character engine](../building-blocks/character-engine.md)).
4. **One-page synopsis.** Turn each sentence of step 2 into a full paragraph. All but the last end in a disaster. This is your skeletal plot.
5. **Character synopses.** Tell the entire story from each major character's point of view so every one has independent agency, not just service to the protagonist.
6. **Four-page synopsis.** Expand each paragraph of step 4 into a page. This is where you make the hard, high-level logic decisions.
7. **Character bible.** Exhaustive dossiers — physical description, history, and exactly how each character changes by the climax.
8. **Scene spreadsheet.** Break the synopsis into a row per scene: POV character, location, and central conflict.
9. **Scene prototypes.** Expand each spreadsheet row into a multi-paragraph sketch — dialogue snippets, sensory beats.
10. **Write the prose.** With structure, psychology, and logistics fully mapped, draft fast and largely block-free.

---

## How to use it to create a book

- Don't skip ahead. The value is in *each* expansion forcing a decision before it's expensive to change.
- Steps 1–4 are cheap and reversible — iterate freely. Step 5 onward is where time investment climbs sharply.
- Treat the disasters in step 2 as the spine. If you can't name three escalating disasters, the premise isn't load-bearing yet.

## Pitfalls

- **Over-planning the joy out of it.** If filling the character bible kills your momentum, stop at step 4 and start drafting — you can return.
- **Rigid lock-in.** When drafting reveals a better path, update the outline rather than forcing the prose to obey a stale plan.
